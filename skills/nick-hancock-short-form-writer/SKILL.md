## ITERATION VS INSPIRATION — READ THIS FIRST

Every brief will fall into one of two modes. Identify the mode before writing a single word.

**Iteration** — the brief says the original performed exceptionally well (outlier views, viral reposts, high saves) and asks you to redo it. The brief might say "iterate this", "keep it mostly the same", or reference the original's performance directly.
- Stay as close to the original as possible — same hook type, same flow, same key phrases where they worked, same structural order.
- Do not apply the standard framework on top of it. The original's structure is the framework for this script.
- Do not upgrade the hook to something "better" by your own judgement. If the original opened with a genuine question, keep a genuine question. If it opened with a specific number, keep a number. The hook worked. Do not change it.
- Changes allowed: clean up rough phrasing, tighten sentences, remove filler, fix anything that clearly doesn't land. But the skeleton stays.

**Inspiration** — the brief references an existing piece of content but is not asking for a direct redo. The goal is a new script that takes cues from the original's tone, topic, or angle.
- Follow the standard framework.
- Blend in elements from the original where they feel natural, but do not be constrained by its structure.

If the brief says the original got exceptional numbers and asks you to "iterate" or "keep it the same" — that is iteration, not inspiration. Do not drift toward the framework.

## AMPLIFIER RULES

The amplifier is the line or two that follows the hook. It is the most misused element in short-form scripting. Get it wrong and you kill the scroll-stop the hook just created.

**What the amplifier must do:**
- Deepen the tension or reframe the belief — make the viewer more curious, not less
- Open a loop, not close one
- Never reveal the answer — that belongs in the body

**What the amplifier must never do:**
- Reveal what the "one thing" is — kills the hook immediately
- Use lines like "most coaches won't tell you this" — triggers sales resistance instantly, sounds unnatural and salesy
- Sound like a teaser for a paid product before trust is established

**The two amplifier moves that work:**

1. **Create a common enemy** — identify an external force (an industry, a system, a widespread belief) that has been misleading the viewer. Builds rapport fast because you are on their side against something, not positioning yourself above other coaches.
   Example: "Because we've been brainwashed by the supplement companies and what they've been marketing in the past decade."

2. **Shatter a comfortable assumption** — identify what the viewer currently blames for their problem, then tell them they're blaming the wrong thing. Controversial without being aggressive.
   Example: "Wrong distance. Wrong sessions. Wrong coach. But the plan isn't the problem. The gap between your plan and your life is."

**The test:** Read the hook and amplifier together. Does the viewer now know what the "one thing" is? If yes, rewrite the amplifier.

## HOOK REFINEMENT PRINCIPLES

**Weak hooks are descriptive. Strong hooks are confrontational or controversial.**
"Your training plan has no idea what your Tuesday looks like" — descriptive, mildly interesting, easy to scroll past.
"I'm sorry to break it to you but if you haven't hit your target in your last ultra, your plan wasn't the problem" — confrontational, breaks a belief they're holding, impossible to scroll past.

**Break a belief, don't confirm one.**
The viewer already believes their plan is wrong, their coach is wrong, their distance was wrong. If the hook confirms that belief, they nod and scroll. If the hook challenges it, they stop.

**Controversy without aggression.**
"I'm sorry to break it to you" softens a confrontational statement without removing the edge. Use it when the hook challenges something the viewer has likely believed for a long time.

**The hook is not the setup. It is the disruption.**
A good hook doesn't set up the video — it disrupts the viewer's current mental state enough that they need to resolve it by watching.

## THE SIX HOOK TYPES

Choose one. The hook is 1-2 sentences maximum. It must land within 2-3 seconds of reading aloud.

Structure when two sentences: sentence one makes the claim or asks the question. Sentence two adds the consequence, the promise, or the stakes.

If Nick is demonstrating something visually, add a bracketed direction note before the hook: `[while demonstrating X]`

**1. Contrarian statement**
Open by saying the opposite of what most runners believe. State it as fact, no question mark.
Example: "Zone 2 heart rate training for new runners does not matter."

**2. Consequence threat**
Open with a mistake + the direct consequence it causes.
Example: "If you're overstriding, you are actively creating a braking force with every single step — and it could be costing you 8 to 10 percent of your running economy."

**3. Knowledge gap**
Open by telling them there's something they don't know, then promise to explain it.
Example: "Most runners have no idea what's actually happening in their legs after kilometre 32 of a marathon. Once you understand it, you'll never train the same way again."

**4. Specific number**
Open with a precise, arresting number or data point. No context yet.
Example: "6 minutes 52 seconds per mile. That's sub-3 pace. But most runners trying to run it don't know whether their body is actually ready to hold it."

**5. Genuine question**
Open with a question the viewer is already asking themselves. Not rhetorical — the question IS the hook.
Example: "So what is the difference between a tempo run and a threshold run — and when should you do one or the other?"

**6. Myth bust**
Open by naming something runners commonly do or believe, then immediately say it won't do what they think.
Example: "More zone 2 isn't going to make you hit a new marathon PB. Not on its own."

## BODY STRUCTURE — TWO FRAMEWORKS

### Framework A: Single-topic reel

Use when the reel explores one idea, one mistake, or one fix in depth.

1. **Context** — give the viewer the background they need to care. What is the situation most runners find themselves in?
2. **Justify the hook** — explain *why* the controversial or surprising thing in the hook is actually true. Back it up with specificity, data, or a mechanism they can visualise.
3. **The epiphany** — the specific, actionable insight that reframes how they think about or do this thing. One concrete change, cue, or realisation.

Optionally: re-hook after the epiphany then deliver a second epiphany. Only use if there is genuinely more value to deliver.

---

### Framework B: Multi-tip reel

Use when the reel delivers three or more distinct tips, mistakes, or tactics.

**Cake-shit-cake:**
1. **Tip 1 — strong** — the most interesting, counterintuitive, or high-value tip goes first.
2. **Tip 2 — can be average** — useful but less exciting middle.
3. **Tip 3 — strong** — end on another high-value tip.

Do not bury the best tips in the middle. Do not end on the least interesting point.

---

Each section does ONE job. Do not pad. Always output a fully scripted body — never bullet points.

**Personal proof (optional — use if Nick provided it):**
Place inside the body, immediately after the section it supports. 1-2 sentences, direct and specific.
Example: "I ran 2:50 at London at 43 following this exact structure."

**Analogy (optional — only if the concept is abstract):**
Use only if the topic involves a mechanism the viewer cannot see or easily feel. Must use something from everyday life, map directly onto the running concept, and be a single sentence.

**Example 1 — Genuine question hook**

HOOK
so what is the difference between a tempo run and a threshold run and when should you do one or the other?

BODY

THE DISTINCTION
Threshold pace is the pace at which lactate accumulation and lactate clearance are balanced. It's a specific physiological point. Tempo running is a broader term — it just means comfortably hard. You can run tempo and never touch threshold. That's wasted adaptation.

WHY IT MATTERS
If you're targeting a specific race pace — say, sub-3 for a marathon — your threshold is the ceiling of your sustainable effort. Training at or slightly below it teaches your body to clear lactate more efficiently. Training at a vague 'comfortably hard' pace doesn't hit the same stimulus.

HOW TO HIT IT PROPERLY
The simplest field test: run at a pace you could sustain for exactly one hour if you went flat out. That's close to your lactate threshold. Structure your threshold sessions around that pace, not around how you feel on the day.

**Example 4 — Consequence threat hook / visual demo**

HOOK
[while overstriding] overstriding can cost you 8 to 10 percent of your running economy and increase the likelihood of injury. here's what it looks like, and how to fix it in one session.

BODY

WHAT IT IS
overstriding means your foot is landing in front of your centre of mass on every step. that's a braking force. every heel-strike in front of your hips is your body fighting itself.

ELITE CONTRAST
elite runners don't try to lengthen their stride to run faster. they increase their cadence. the average elite marathon runner turns over at 180 steps per minute or above.

THE FIX
you don't need to overhaul your entire form. increase your cadence by 5 steps per minute each week until you reach 170-175 steps per minute. set a metronome at your current cadence plus 5 on your next easy run. your stride will naturally shorten, your foot will land closer to your centre of gravity, and ground contact time drops.

overstriding is like trying to drive faster by pressing the brake and accelerator at the same time.
